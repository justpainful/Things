Verdict

E — Things GlassD — SVG displacementC — CSS rim-lensB — Vibrant slabA — Naive blur

E is the closest to Apple's material, and it is not close.

The thing that makes Liquid Glass read as an object rather than a translucent rectangle is that the background behind it is geometrically wrong at the rim and right in the middle. A real convex lozenge has near-zero surface slope across its face and a steep slope in the last few millimetres before the edge, so light entering near the rim gets bent hard: you see a squeezed, displaced, slightly magnified band of whatever is behind and just outside the shape, and then the centre snaps back to almost-clear. Only D and E reproduce that, because only they actually resample the backdrop per-pixel. Everything else is painting over the backdrop rather than bending it.

E beats D on four counts, all of which are visible on the stripe backdrop:

  • Chromatic dispersion. Real glass has a different refractive index per wavelength. E runs three displacement passes at ±6% scale and recombines the R, G and B channels, so the rim band picks up a faint warm/cool split. It is subtle to the point of subliminal, and removing it is instantly noticeable — the rim goes from "glass" to "smear".
  • Adaptive contrast. contrast(0.78) inside the backdrop chain pulls the backdrop toward mid grey — whites come down, blacks come up — and a trailing saturate() puts back the chroma that costs. Over the white stripes the glass darkens, over the black stripes it lifts, and the label contrast ratio stops swinging. B and D both fail the stripe test at the text; E holds. Worth saying plainly: the mix-blend-mode: luminosity layer that every tutorial reaches for here cannot work. A blended child isolates its parent, an isolated group is a backdrop root, and every backdrop-filter in the subtree silently resolves to nothing. That bug was live in this page for one build; the filter-chain version has no such conflict and is cheaper.
  • Belt and braces at the rim. E keeps a narrow CSS rim-lens ring on top of the SVG displacement. On Chromium the two reinforce each other; on Safari and Firefox — where backdrop-filter: url(#…) is parsed and then silently ignored — the ring is the only refraction left, and it carries the effect on its own. D degrades to plain B there with no warning.
  • Interaction. Pointer-tracked specular, displacement scale ramping ~35% on press, and a 1.4% compression. The material responds by changing shape and light, not by changing colour — which is the correct read of what iOS does.

Why A must be rejected

A is heavy blur plus a flat white fill. Three separate tells: the blur radius is roughly 2× what Apple uses, so the backdrop turns to mush and the glass reads as frosted plastic; there is no saturation lift, so colours behind it go duller when real glass makes them more alive; and the edge is a hard alpha cut with no border, no arc and no shadow, so it has no thickness and does not float. Put A next to E over the mesh backdrop and A looks like a screenshot with a bug.

What to ship

Adopt E for the Things web client, authored as a .tg-glass component with the layer stack intact. Ship C as the automatic degradation for non-Chromium engines — it is the same visual grammar minus the per-pixel resample, and users will not see the two side by side. Below that, prefers-reduced-transparency collapses everything to an opaque surface with a 1px border and the layout does not move a pixel. Details, cost and rollout notes are in NOTES.md next to this file.
